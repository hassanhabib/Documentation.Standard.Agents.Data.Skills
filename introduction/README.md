# Introduction

## Natural Language Is the New Programming Language

For seventy years, to instruct a machine you learned its language. You wrote in symbols
the machine could execute, and the discipline of software engineering grew up around that
act: how to structure code, how to name it, how to keep it from collapsing under its own
weight.

That era is not over, but a second one has begun on top of it. The machine can now be
instructed in the language you already speak. You no longer write every step; you describe
the intent, and an agent carries it out. In this Software Engineering 2.0, natural language
is the new programming language, and prose is the new source code.

A **skill** is a program written in that language. It is a unit of behavior, authored in
words, that tells an agent how to act. And because the language is prose, its power is
startling and its danger is intimate: a single sentence can steer an agent one way or the
other. "Always confirm before you delete," and the absence of that sentence, are a few
words apart, and they are the whole distance between a careful assistant and one that
empties a drive without asking. The instruction that once took a module now takes a
sentence. So does the mistake.

## Why Skills Must Be Standardized

A sentence is not a specification. The same words mean different things to different
models, in different contexts, at different moments. Interpretation is variance, and
variance is precisely what you cannot afford when the stakes are high.

In a low-stakes setting, a skill that is read two ways is a quirk. In surgery, in risk
evaluation, in construction, a skill that is read two ways is a body count. Leaving a
life-critical instruction to the raw interpretation of a language model is leaving it to
chance, and chance is not a safety strategy.

Standardization is how you take the chance out. A standard makes a skill precise instead of
suggestive, testable instead of hopeful, certified instead of assumed. It says what a skill
must guarantee, refuses what cannot be verified, and keeps a human answerable at the moment
harm turns grave. The thesis of everything that follows is short: a skill is authored, not
left to interpretation.

## The Standard, and Why a New One

I wrote The Standard for Software Engineering because software needed order: a shared way to
structure, name, and reason about the code we build. I thought a handful of people would
read it. Thousands now build by it, and that has taught me how much weight a standard comes
to carry once people rely on it.

That Standard was written for a world where humans wrote the code. That world is changing
under our feet. When the source becomes prose and the author becomes an agent, the old
Standard remains necessary but is no longer sufficient. The craft has a new surface, and the
new surface needs its own discipline.

So this is a new Standard for a new era: for Agents, for the Skills they consume, for the
Memories they carry, and for everything else that Software Engineering 2.0 is assembling.
This document is the first member of that family, the Standard for Agent Skills. It does not
replace the first Standard; it extends the same conviction into the place the work has moved.

## Blessing or Curse

Artificial intelligence is not good or evil. It is powerful, and power takes its meaning
from its use. The same skill is a blessing or a curse depending on how it is used, where it
is used, and who is using it. On the right model, in careful hands, toward a worthy end, it
heals. On the wrong model, in careless or hostile hands, it harms. Often the entire distance
between the two is a single uncertified sentence.

A standard is what makes that distance legible and governable. It is the seatbelt, not the
engine: it does not decide where you go, but it decides whether a mistake is survivable.
Written with rigor, it is among the most protective things we can build for this era.
Written carelessly, it is a rubber stamp on catastrophe, carrying the authority of a
standard while offering none of the safety.

Which of the two it becomes is not decided by the technology. It is decided by the care we
bring to it. That is why this Standard exists, and it is why it is held, from its first word
to its last, as a trust.
